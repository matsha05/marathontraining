## 1) Starrett’s mobility standards concept
Starrett’s “Ready to Run” framing is: **running durability is gated by movement standards**. If you cannot hit baseline positions cleanly, your body compensates under load and you get injured. He organizes this as **12 standards** that function like a readiness checklist. ([DrChrisBakerDC][1])
A practical summary list of those “12 Standards” (as commonly summarized) is:
1. Neutral Feet
2. Flat Shoes
3. A Supple Thoracic Spine
4. Efficient Squatting Technique
5. Hip Flexion
6. Hip Extension
7. Ankle Range of Motion
8. Warming Up / Cooling Down
9. Compression Socks
10. No Hotspots
11. Hydration
12. Jumping / Landing ([DrChrisBakerDC][1])
### Starrett’s “daily checks + daily maintenance” (how it behaves in a program)
**Daily check idea:** you should be able to quickly confirm:
* “Can I hit the positions today?”
* “Do I have any hotspots that will change my mechanics?”
* “Do I need 10 to 15 minutes of maintenance before I go smash myself?”
He’s explicit that small daily work matters: he recommends **10 to 15 minutes a day** on these basics. ([Outside Online][2])
### What he’s really doing, in operational terms
Starrett’s system decomposes into 3 daily actions:
1. **Position checks (can you assume key shapes?)**
   * Example: couch stretch shape, squat shape, ankle dorsiflexion/pistol “end shape.”
2. **Tissue quality maintenance (do tissues glide?)**
   * Example: calf smash/pressure wave style tissue work. ([Outside Online][2])
3. **Movement practice (reinforce correct mechanics under light load)**
   * Example: squat practice emphasizing glutes, heel-down depth, knee tracking. ([Outside Online][2])

## 2) Dicharry’s runner-specific stability and strength concepts
Dicharry’s “Running Rewired” lens is: running injuries are often a **skill and control problem**, not only a mobility problem. The theme is **precision movement + stability under single-leg load**, especially through the foot, pelvis, and trunk.
### Core concepts to encode
**A) Foot tripod and big-toe control as a foundation**
Dicharry repeatedly ties durability to big-toe driven foot stability and torsional control. In his Achilles-focused writing, he explains that you must control torsional displacement through the foot and that this is controlled primarily by the first ray (big toe). ([RUN | Powered by Outside][3])
That same thread shows up in his “Toe Yoga / Foot Control Test” work. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][4])
**B) Deep core stabilizers (alignment and precision), not “six-pack flexion work”**
He explicitly contrasts endurance needs with sit-up style core work and focuses on deep stabilizers for alignment and precision. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][5])
**C) Hip extension must happen without stealing from the low back**
He emphasizes driving hips into extension while keeping the spine stable, because many athletes extend their low back instead. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][6])
**D) Mobility is only “necessary” when it’s limiting function**
His hip mobility doorway test excerpt is very clear: if you feel a huge pull in front of the thigh, incorporate the kneeling hip flexor stretch. If you feel nothing or gentle lengthening, you likely don’t need static hip flexor stretching. ([Women's Running][7])
That is a very “encodeable” decision rule.

## 3) Key assessment tests for durability
Below is a battery built from the most directly-quotable/self-testable items in the sources we pulled. It blends:
* Starrett-style “standards”
* Dicharry-style “control + capacity” checks
* Outside’s “Measurable Goals” article (which includes multiple Dicharry-sourced tests and runner durability targets) ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
### A. Daily “readiness” quick checks (1 to 2 minutes total)
These are fast, low friction, and map directly to module selection:
1. **Toe Yoga control check**
   If you cannot raise big toe while other toes stay down, then reverse without collapsing arch, your foot control is limiting. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][4])
2. **Single-leg balance time (eyes open, barefoot, hands on shoulders)**
   Targets by age are provided (for ages 18-39: 45s). ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
3. **Squat shape check (5 reps, filmed or mirrored)**
   Look for heel-down balance, knee tracking, control, pain/tension location. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
### B. Weekly “durability dashboard” (10 to 12 minutes total)
These give you objective gates and progression targets:
1. **Single-leg squat control**
   Goal: 10 to 20 reps to about 1/4 depth with control. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
2. **Hip extension capacity (target ~20 degrees)**
   This is listed as a mobility goal; improving it ties to hip flexor mobility, glute strength, and core stability. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
3. **Ankle dorsiflexion knee-to-wall**
   Protocol: toes about 4 to 5 inches from wall, knee touches wall without heel lift and knee tracks over second toe. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
4. **Hallux (big toe) dorsiflexion**
   Goal: ~50 to 70 degrees; quick self-check is flattening the big toe in kneeling position. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
5. **Single-leg calf raise capacity**
   Goal: at least 20 continuous pain-free reps with minimal side discrepancy. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
6. **Single-leg bridge (hip extension + core stability)**
   Hold 10 seconds, hips level, feel glute doing work, no hamstring cramp or low-back pain. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
7. **Squat repeats**
   Goal: 20 full-depth, pain-free squats. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
8. **Push-ups**
   Goal: men 10, women 5 with good form (also tied to posture + big toe position). ([RUN | Powered by Outside][8])
9. **Dicharry hip mobility doorway test**
   Interpretation rule plus linked corrective stretch guidance. ([Women's Running][7])
10. **Starrett ankle ROM “pistol end shape” concept**
    A commonly summarized Ready to Run ankle dorsiflexion standard is being able to assume a pistol bottom position without heel lift or arch collapse. ([DrChrisBakerDC][1])

## 4) Recommended daily prehab routine structure
You want something that is:
* **Short**
* **Assessment-driven**
* **Composable** (modules)
* **Retestable** (so you can stop doing things once they are “good enough”)
Here’s a structure that matches both authors:
### Daily micro-dose template (8 to 12 minutes)
1. **Readiness scan (1 to 2 min)**
   * Toe Yoga check (30 to 45s)
   * Single-leg balance quick check (10 to 20s each side)
   * 5 squats (20 to 30s)
2. **1 mobility module (3 to 5 min)**
   * Only for the joint/position that fails today
     Starrett explicitly recommends small daily time investments like this. ([Outside Online][2])
     Dicharry also uses “only stretch if test says you need it” logic. ([Women's Running][7])
3. **1 control/stability module (3 to 5 min)**
   * Foot tripod or deep core or hip control
4. **Optional: 1 capacity micro-dose (2 to 4 min)**
   * Calf raises or bridges, depending on what is lagging
### Frequency intent (simple rule set)
* **Foot control and deep core**: frequent, low dose. Dicharry specifically recommends control exercises 5 to 10 minutes, 2 to 3 times per week in his Achilles-focused guidance, but you can also split that into micro-doses. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][3])
* **Static stretching**: only when a test indicates tightness (Dicharry hip flexor test rule). ([Women's Running][7])
* **Tissue work**: small doses when hotspots are present, or after harder runs.

## 5) How to prioritize mobility vs stability work based on assessments
This is the part you can encode as routing logic.
### Priority rules (the “why”)
1. **Pain/hotspot overrides everything**
   If a pattern is painful or you have a hotspot that changes mechanics, route to tissue + load reduction first (Starrett’s “no hotspots” standard). ([DrChrisBakerDC][1])
2. **Foundation first: foot and ankle control/mobility**
   If Toe Yoga fails or ankle DF is limited, fix that before chasing hip/glute strength. Dicharry explicitly links big toe control to foot stability and Achilles loading. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][3])
3. **Then: hip extension without lumbar compensation**
   If you cannot extend the hip cleanly, fix hip flexor tightness and glute/core strategy (couch stretch plus bridge/dead bug style work). ([Outside Online][2])
4. **Then: trunk control (deep core, anti-rotation)**
   If side plank / deep core patterns are weak, your pelvis and trunk will leak while running. ([RUN | Powered by Outside][5])
5. **Finally: capacity targets**
   Once positions and control are acceptable, build calf capacity, squat capacity, hopping/landing progressions.
### A clean routing heuristic you can implement
* If a test **fails**, assign it a severity score:
  * 2 = pain
  * 1 = cannot do movement to standard
  * 0 = passes
* Sort by:
  1. pain first
  2. distal to proximal (foot/ankle before hip before trunk)
  3. biggest left-right asymmetry
* Choose:
  * 1 mobility module for the highest-ranked mobility fail
  * 1 control module for the highest-ranked control fail
  * optionally 1 capacity module if no pain and at least 80% of control tests pass
